# Cover letter — submission of the C8 manuscript

**To the Editors, *Anesthesiology*** (alternative target: *British Journal of Anaesthesia*)

Dear Editors,

We submit for your consideration our Original Investigation, **"Oscillometric Cuff Monitoring Systematically
Underestimates Intraoperative Hypotension and Its Harms: A Two-Cohort Study with US Multi-Center External
Validation."**

**The problem.** The entire intraoperative-hypotension evidence base — the effect sizes, dose-response curves, and
the MAP < 65 mmHg treatment thresholds now embedded in perioperative guidelines — rests almost entirely on
intermittent oscillometric (cuff) blood pressure, because arterial lines are reserved for higher-risk cases.
Oscillometric devices are known to be inaccurate at low pressures, yet the downstream consequences of that error
for the field's conclusions have not been quantified against hard outcomes.

**What we show.** Using two surgical cohorts that uniquely co-record cuff and arterial-line MAP, and a
regression-to-the-mean-safe design that fixes the arterial line as the reference, we find the cuff detects only
~56% of arterial-defined hypotension (missing 73% of the most severe episodes), over-reading by ~30 mmHg at low
pressure. Critically, this misclassification **attenuates the measured hypotension–harm association** — within the
same operations, adjusted odds ratios for mortality, hyperlactatemia, AKI, a composite, and ICU admission are
substantially larger when hypotension is measured by arterial line than by cuff. A tautology analysis shows the
attenuation is the exact, quantified consequence of undercounting (the cuff flag carries no harm information once
true arterial exposure is modeled), and a within-operation matched-cadence analysis localizes it to measurement
bias rather than sampling frequency. A vasopressor-stratified within-patient crossover establishes the low-pressure
over-read as intrinsic oscillometric physics, not a treatment artifact. The discrepancy and its direction of
harm-attenuation **replicate in an independent US multi-center ICU cohort (eICU, 154 hospitals)**, and the specific
threshold operating characteristics replicate across 1.14 million paired readings.

**Why it matters.** Two clinically actionable implications follow. First, effect sizes and "safe" MAP thresholds
derived from cuff data warrant upward revision — the field has likely underestimated the true harm of intraoperative
hypotension. Second, where arterial monitoring is unavailable, a corrected cuff treatment threshold (MAP < 70)
recovers most of the missed hypotension; we present this as a testable candidate correction (with its false-positive
cost reported honestly and its net benefit reserved for a randomized trial, whose protocol accompanies this work).

**Rigor and honesty.** This work was hardened through three rounds of adversarial internal review. We report a
prediction-model analysis that **failed** external validation and label it exploratory rather than bury it; we
state the single-institution limitation of the primary cohorts explicitly; and every number traces to a specific,
reproducible analysis (appendix included). We make no causal claim about arterial-line placement improving
outcomes — that is the subject of the companion trial.

We believe the measurement finding — that the instrument underlying an entire guideline literature systematically
misses the exposure it is meant to detect — is of broad importance to anesthesiologists and intensivists. The
manuscript has not been published elsewhere and is not under consideration by another journal. All authors have
approved the submission and declare no conflicts of interest (to confirm).

Thank you for your consideration.

Sincerely,
The authors
